# We Already Have Socialism in America. It's Just for the Rich. And It's Costing Us the Future.

*Draft 3 — September 29, 2026. Every number is linked to a primary or major-outlet source, with a preference for data released in the last 12 months. Fact-check table and editorial notes at the bottom.*

---

In 1968, Martin Luther King Jr. stood in front of a crowd at Hunter College and said something that still lands like a punch: ["This country has socialism for the rich, rugged individualism for the poor."](https://cityobservatory.org/dr-king-socialism-for-the-rich-and-rugged-free-enterprise-capitalism-for-the-poor/)

He was talking about suburbs built on federally subsidized credit and highways built with 90% federal money, while poor families were told to pull themselves up. When the government helped the rich, it was called a subsidy. When it helped the poor, it was called welfare.

Fifty-eight years later, the subsidies have changed. The pattern hasn't. And in 2026 we have better data than King ever had to prove it.

Here's the simplest way I can put it. When the people at the top of the economy take a risk and it blows up, the losses get spread across all of us. When the bet pays off, the profits stay private. When a family at the bottom takes a hit, they get a 25% interest rate and a repo man.

I've written before about why I think [the K-shaped economy is here to stay](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1mkVJuvFGPEtQxVfwWNF8Ps4chrGmZAkVYmjX1YKaPU4/edit). This piece is about the machine underneath it, and about what that machine is doing to the country's future. Because the K didn't happen by accident. A lot of it is policy. And a lot of that policy moves money in exactly one direction.

I'm not anti-business. I run a nonprofit and I've built a company. I'm not selling fear either. I just think we should call things what they are.

## Quick Background

If you need the K-shape refresher, here it is in five numbers, all of them from the last few months.

The top 1% of American households now hold [32.5% of all U.S. wealth](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/WFRBST01134), per Federal Reserve data released September 18. That's a new record. The bottom 50%? [2.3%](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/WFRBSB50215). Combined.

Households headed by someone under 40 hold [6.6% of the country's net worth](https://eco3min.fr/en/share-of-us-wealth-by-generation/). When the Fed started tracking it in 1989, that number was 12%. Young America's share of the pie has been cut nearly in half.

The share of economic output going to workers just hit [52.8%](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2026/labor-share-at-its-lowest-level-52-8-percent-in-second-quarter-2026.htm), the lowest level since the Bureau of Labor Statistics started tracking it in 1947. Back then it was 65.5%.

The stock market keeps hitting records. The top 1% own [about half of all corporate equities and mutual fund shares](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/z1/dataviz/dfa/distribute/table/). The bottom 50% own about 1%. And S&P 500 companies just spent a record [$1.02 trillion buying back their own stock](https://press.spglobal.com/2025-12-18-S-P-500-Q3-2025-Buybacks-Post-Modest-6-2-Gain-to-249-0-Billion-After-Declining-20-1-Amidst-Uncertainty-in-Q2-Q4-2025-Expenditures-Expected-to-Post-Similar-Growth,-As-2025-Anticipates-a-Record-1-Trillion) in a single year. Goldman expects [$1.4 trillion in 2026](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/goldman-sachs-sees-1-4-143200767.html).

And how do people feel about it? Consumer sentiment fell to [48.1 in September](https://spectrumlocalnews.com/mi/michigan/news/2026/09/25/consumer-sentiment-survey-michigan), down 15% since January. The University of Michigan noted that lower- and middle-income consumers and people without stock holdings saw the sharpest deterioration.

Those are the outcomes. Now let's talk about the mechanisms.

## The Latest Version Was Just Signed Into Law

Before we go through the case studies, look at the single clearest piece of evidence we've ever had.

In August 2025, the Congressional Budget Office analyzed who wins and who loses from the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, the biggest tax and spending law in years. The answer: households in [the bottom 10% lose about $1,200 a year. Households in the top 10% gain about $13,600 a year](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61367). Every year, through 2034.

That's not my analysis. That's Congress's own nonpartisan scorekeeper.

The losses at the bottom come from roughly [$1 trillion in Medicaid cuts](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61570) and [$186 billion in SNAP cuts](https://www.cbpp.org/research/food-assistance/by-the-numbers-senate-republican-leaderships-reconciliation-bill-takes), the largest cut to food assistance in the program's history. The gains at the top come from tax cuts.

It's already happening. USDA data shows [nearly 3.5 million people left SNAP between July 2025 and January 2026](https://www.governing.com/policy/map-snap-enrollment-has-dropped-in-every-state) as the new work-requirement paperwork rolled out. Yale and Penn researchers estimated the House version of the bill could cause [more than 51,000 preventable deaths a year](https://ldi.upenn.edu/our-work/research-updates/research-memo-projected-mortality-impacts-of-the-budget-reconciliation-bill/), including 13,000 in nursing homes from rolling back staffing rules. A later peer-reviewed study in [JAMA Health Forum](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12268486/) put the number from coverage loss alone far lower, around 1,500 a year. Pick whichever estimate you trust. Neither one is zero.

Now here's the pattern that bill fits into.

## 1. Bailouts: Heads They Win, Tails You Pay

In the decade before the pandemic, the biggest U.S. airlines spent [96% of their free cash flow buying back their own stock](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2020-03-16/u-s-airlines-spent-96-of-free-cash-flow-on-buybacks-chart). American Airlines had *negative* cumulative free cash flow over that decade and still repurchased [more than $12.5 billion](https://www.dallasnews.com/business/airlines/2020/03/18/american-airlines-spent-12-billion-on-stock-buybacks-during-flush-times-now-it-says-it-needs-a-bailout/) of its own shares.

Then COVID hit. Within weeks, Congress created the Payroll Support Program. Three rounds later, passenger airlines had received [$54 billion](https://home.treasury.gov/policy-issues/coronavirus/assistance-for-american-industry/payroll-support-program). The only real string: no buybacks or dividends for a while.

"A while" ended in fall 2022. Southwest [reinstated its dividend that December](https://www.marketbeat.com/articles/southwest-airlines-brings-back-dividend-as-travel-rebounds-2022-12-07). Delta [followed in June 2023](https://www.ajc.com/news/atlanta-airport-blog/delta-to-resume-dividend-payouts-with-cares-act-restrictions-lifted/FJW5E4D4FBEOBGF3GPCSERKOIY/). United announced a [$1.5 billion buyback in October 2024](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/10/15/united-airlines-ual-3q-2024-earnings.html). The unions [begged them not to](https://www.cnbc.com/2022/08/18/airline-unions-urge-carriers-not-to-resume-buybacks-when-bailout-ban-ends-this-fall.html). They did it anyway. In 2025, United's CEO made [$32.3 million](https://onemileatatime.com/insights/highest-paid-airline-ceo/).

Meanwhile, Spirit Airlines, one of the cheapest ways for a working-class family to fly, [shut down in May 2026](https://www.cnn.com/2026/05/02/business/spirit-to-halt-all-flights) and took 17,000 jobs with it. Nobody wrote Spirit a check.

The airlines weren't even the biggest version. The Paycheck Protection Program handed out roughly $800 billion. Economists led by MIT's David Autor found that only [23 to 34 cents of every PPP dollar](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fjep.36.2.55) reached workers who would otherwise have lost their jobs. The rest went to business owners, shareholders, creditors and suppliers. Of the 2020 money, 72% flowed to the top fifth of households. Cost per job-year saved: $169,000 to $258,000.

In March 2023, Silicon Valley Bank collapsed. Deposits are insured up to $250,000. Overnight, the government [guaranteed every dollar](https://www.fdic.gov/news/press-releases/2023/pr23019.html), including the uninsured balances of venture-backed startups and wealthy depositors. The [$16.3 billion](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2023/11/29/2023-25813/special-assessment-pursuant-to-systemic-risk-determination) bill got charged to the banking system.

And in October 2025, the Treasury used its Exchange Stabilization Fund to hand Argentina a [$20 billion currency swap](https://www.cnn.com/2025/10/12/economy/argentina-america-bailout-currency), propping up the peso. Billionaire investors who had [publicly bet on Argentina](https://abcnews.com/Politics/trump-administrations-20b-bailout-argentina/story?id=126513232) after its election were among those whose positions the swap protected. The Treasury Secretary called the idea that this helped wealthy Americans "a trope." American soybean farmers, watching Argentina sell to China while their own crop sat unsold from the trade war, [had a different word for it](https://abcnews.com/Politics/trump-administrations-20b-bailout-argentina/story?id=126513232).

Here's the thing. Every one of these had a defensible argument in the moment. I'm not saying let the planes fall out of the sky. I'm saying notice how fast the money moved, and who it landed on. And notice that record buybacks are the sequel to every one of them.

## 2. Long-Term Care: The Owners Get Paid Either Way

This is the one almost nobody talks about, and it's the one that hits working and middle-class families hardest. It's also the clearest case of the whole pattern, so I'm going to slow down on it.

Start with a fact most Americans don't learn until it's too late: [Medicare does not cover long-term care](https://www.medicare.gov/coverage/nursing-home-care). It pays for up to 100 days of skilled nursing after a hospital stay. It pays nothing for the help with bathing, eating and dressing that most nursing home residents actually need. Zero.

And most of us will need it. [56% of Americans turning 65 today](https://rooseveltinstitute.org/publications/how-long-term-care-costs-drain-the-middle-class/) will need long-term care. One in five will need it for five years or more. A private nursing home room now costs a median of [$129,575 a year](https://investor.genworth.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/1054/carescout-releases-2025-cost-of-care-survey-results). Median household income for Americans over 65 is about $57,000.

So here's the ladder a middle-class family climbs down.

**Step one: pay until you're broke.** You write checks from a lifetime of savings. A 2025 study found that among residents who entered paying privately, [one in six spent down to Medicaid during their stay, in an average of just 6.1 months](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12679321/). Six months to burn through what took forty years to build.

**Step two: qualify as poor.** In most states, Medicaid won't pay until you have [no more than $2,000 in countable assets](https://www.medicaidlongtermcare.org/eligibility/spend-down/). The Roosevelt Institute found that [more than four out of five middle-class adults who need five-plus years of care end up on Medicaid](https://rooseveltinstitute.org/publications/how-long-term-care-costs-drain-the-middle-class/). After care begins, middle-class families keep about 42% of their wealth. The top quarter keeps 94%.

**Step three: the state takes the house.** When you die, federal law [requires states to recover what Medicaid spent from your estate](https://www.medicaid.gov/medicaid/eligibility-policy/estate-recovery). For most working families, the estate is the house. The one asset they had to pass down. Gone.

Now here's the part that should make you angry. The rich don't climb that ladder.

If you have money and foresight, you hire an elder-law attorney, move your assets into an irrevocable trust, wait out Medicaid's [five-year lookback](https://www.elderlawanswers.com/medicaid-and-trusts-12004), and qualify for Medicaid with your wealth intact. It's legal. It's routine. It costs [$3,000 to $15,000 in legal fees](https://www.payingforseniorcare.com/find_medicaid_planning_help), which the industry's own marketing describes as "less than one month of nursing home care." An elder-care lawyer put it plainly on his own blog: [funding long-term care "is really a problem for the middle and working classes, not the rich."](https://www.eldercarelawyer.com/blog/medicaid-planning-for-the-rich/)

Read that again. The public program for the poor is available to the wealthy through a lawyer, and unavailable to the middle class until they've been stripped to $2,000.

So who's on the other end of all that money?

Medicaid is the primary payer for [63% of nursing home residents](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/5-key-facts-about-nursing-facilities-and-medicaid/). It paid 61% of the $459 billion the country spent on long-term care in 2023. And [73% of nursing homes are for-profit](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/a-look-at-nursing-facility-characteristics/). Public money, flowing to private owners. And a lot of it doesn't stay in the building.

**The related-party game.** Nearly three-quarters of U.S. nursing homes, more than 11,000, do business with [companies owned by the same people who own the nursing home](https://kffhealthnews.org/aging/care-suffers-as-more-nursing-homes-feed-money-into-corporate-webs/). The home pays "rent" to its owner's real estate company and "management fees" to its owner's management company. Homes that do this have fewer nurses per patient, higher injury rates, and almost twice the complaints. A 2024 NBER paper found that nursing homes hide [at least 63% of their profits](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w32258/w32258.pdf) this way. The books say the home is barely breaking even. The owner's other companies say otherwise.

**The landlords.** Wall Street figured this out. Real estate investment trusts now hold stakes in [one in six U.S. nursing homes](https://kffhealthnews.org/health-industry/real-estate-investment-trusts-senior-housing-nursing-homes-profit/), and healthcare REITs paid out more than $7 billion in dividends in 2024. One of the biggest, CareTrust, booked a 67% profit margin: $320 million in net income on $476 million in revenue. KFF Health News found one of its nursing homes paying the REIT more than $1 million a year in rent while the home itself ran at a deficit, and found that homes in these portfolios delivered a half hour to more than an hour *less* nursing care per resident per day than the national average. The REITs' lawyers say they're "the property owners, not the operators." The residents' lawyers say "the REITs are in charge." The residents in that story died of bedsores.

**The funds.** A landmark study of more than 7 million Medicare patients found that when a private equity firm buys a nursing home, [short-term mortality goes up 10%](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28474). About 21,000 lives over the study period. At the same time, spending per patient went up 19%, "the vast majority of which is billed to taxpayers."

Let that sink in. They got paid more. The patients died more. And the check came from the public.

**The rulebook.** In 2024, the federal government finally set a minimum staffing standard. The owners' lobby, the American Health Care Association, [spent nearly $17 million lobbying since 2020](https://www.warren.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/warren-schakowsky-slam-largest-nursing-home-lobbying-groups-for-sabotaging-biden-administrations-minimum-nurse-staffing-rules-that-improve-quality-of-care-for-seniors). In December 2025, [the rule was repealed](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/12/03/2025-21792/medicare-and-medicaid-programs-repeal-of-minimum-staffing-standards-for-long-term-care-facilities).

I want to be precise about who I'm describing. The nursing assistant turning your mother every two hours makes a median of [$20 an hour](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/nursing-assistants.htm). Turnover in that job runs [close to 100% a year](https://www.phinational.org/policy-research/key-facts-faq/). She is not the problem. She's getting squeezed by the same structure the residents are. I'm talking about the people above her: the operator who pays rent to himself, the REIT booking a 67% margin on a building where residents get an hour less care, the fund that raised billing 19% while mortality rose 10%.

A landlord who collects rent from a nursing home that's running a deficit, with Medicaid covering the gap and the residents getting less care, isn't running a business. He's running a toll booth on the last years of someone's life. And the toll is paid twice: first by the family, then by the taxpayer.

Follow the money one more time. Grandma's house becomes Medicaid's reimbursement. Medicaid's reimbursement becomes the operator's revenue. The operator's revenue becomes rent to the owner's real estate company. The real estate company is owned by a fund or a REIT. The fund's investors are, overwhelmingly, the top of the K. And the one family that could have protected the house was the one rich enough to hire the lawyer.

None of this is inevitable, by the way. The United States is [one of the few wealthy countries without universal long-term care insurance](https://harkininstitute.drake.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/103/2025/05/LTC-2025-Policy-Brief_v4.pdf). Germany and Japan run mandatory public programs that everyone pays into and everyone can use, no spend-down required. We passed one in 2010, the CLASS Act. Congress [repealed it in January 2013](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R40842.html) before it ever enrolled a single person. The "go broke first" rule is a policy choice, and we already chose against the alternative.

The owners get paid whether the care is good or not. Actually, the data says they get paid *more* when it isn't.

## 3. Grocery Stores and SNAP: Subsidized at Both Ends

SNAP is one of the most effective anti-hunger programs in the world. I want to be clear about that up front. In fiscal 2025 it helped [42.1 million people a month](https://www.ers.usda.gov/topics/food-nutrition-assistance/supplemental-nutrition-assistance-program-snap/key-statistics-and-research) with an average benefit of $188 per person. Total cost: [$101.7 billion](https://usafacts.org/answers/how-much-does-the-federal-government-spend-on-snap-every-year/country/united-states/).

Now look at where those dollars go.

Walmart captures [roughly a quarter of all SNAP grocery spending](https://www.grocerydive.com/news/snap-grocery-spending-5-big-numbers/804627/), triple Kroger's share. A quarter of $95 billion in benefits is somewhere around $23 billion a year moving through Walmart's registers. Walmart's most recent fiscal year: [$713 billion in revenue and $22.3 billion in profit](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/104169/000010416926000032/earningsreleasefy26q4.htm). The Walton family, which owns about 44% of the company, is worth [$475 billion](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/01/16/walton-family-net-worth-rises-32-billion-this-year/).

That's one end. Here's the other.

In June 2026, the Government Accountability Office published its second study of who employs America's safety-net recipients. In 2024, [13.8 million working adults were on Medicaid and 10.6 million were in working SNAP households](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108703). Two-thirds worked full-time. These aren't people who won't work. They're people whose full-time job doesn't pay enough to eat.

Who employs them? Across the nine states GAO could get SNAP data from, [Walmart topped the list with 15,515 workers on food stamps](https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/08/13/grocery-retailers-scrutinized-over-employee-public-assistance/), followed by Amazon, Dollar General, Dollar Tree and Kroger. Amazon's count nearly tripled since 2020. Senator Sanders' office, which commissioned the report, noted that Walmart's profits rose about 47% since 2020 while Medicaid enrollment among its workers rose 55%.

So here's the loop. A cashier's wages don't cover groceries. Taxpayers cover the gap through SNAP. The cashier walks to the front of the store and spends that SNAP money at the register. Taxpayers have now subsidized the payroll *and* the sales of the same company. Twice.

And when Congress cut SNAP, the cut landed on the cashier. Not on the margin.

I'm not saying Walmart designed it this way. I'm saying that when a company can pay below the cost of living and the public fills the gap, the public is doing the company's payroll for it. There's a word for a government program that props up private profit. It just isn't the word we use when the recipient is rich.

## 4. Private Prisons: Guaranteed Customers, Guaranteed Revenue

Every business wants guaranteed demand. Only one industry has it written into a government contract.

When In the Public Interest analyzed more than 60 private prison contracts, [65% contained occupancy guarantees](https://inthepublicinterest.org/criminal-how-lockup-quotas-and-low-crime-taxes-guarantee-profits-for-private-prison-corporations/). The most common: 90%. Three Arizona prisons: 100%. If crime falls and beds go empty, the state pays for them anyway. Think about the incentive that creates.

Immigration detention is where the model has gone fully vertical. As of early 2025, [86% of ICE detainees were held in for-profit facilities](https://tracreports.org/reports/753/). The One Big Beautiful Bill Act added [$45 billion for detention](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/budget-bill-massively-increases-funding-immigration-detention), a 265% increase, enough for roughly 116,000 beds by 2029. More than the entire federal prison system.

The two biggest contractors had the best year in their history. GEO Group's 2025 revenue hit [$2.63 billion, and net income went from $32 million to $254 million](https://investors.geogroup.com/news-releases/news-release-details/geo-group-reports-fourth-quarter-and-full-year-2025-results). Its founder called it "the largest amount of new business we have won in a single year in our company's history." CoreCivic's federal revenue [rose 19% to $1.19 billion](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1070985/000119312526060669/cxw-20251231.htm). Its CEO told shareholders: "Never in our 42-year company history have we had so much activity and demand for our services."

Both companies gave [$500,000 to the 2025 inaugural committee](https://abcnews.com/US/private-prison-firm-corecivic-gave-500k-trumps-inauguration/story?id=118218707), double what they gave in 2017. With their PACs and subsidiaries, they donated [$2.78 million](https://www.citizensforethics.org/reports-investigations/crew-investigations/trumps-budget-bill-benefits-private-immigration-detention-companies-that-donated-to-trump/) to the campaign, the inaugural and related entities. Then the budget passed. Then the stocks went up.

Inside the walls, incarcerated workers nationwide earn [an average of 13 to 52 cents an hour](https://www.aclu.org/publications/captive-labor-exploitation-incarcerated-workers). Seven states pay nothing at all.

This isn't a market. A market has customers who can walk away. This is a public agency guaranteeing a private company's demand with taxpayer money, and the product is a human being in a cell.

## 5. Meanwhile, the Bottom of the K Is Financing Itself With Debt

Now flip the K over. When the top gets in trouble, the interest rate is zero and the government shows up in two weeks. Here's what happens when the bottom gets in trouble.

Total household debt hit [$18.8 trillion](https://www.newyorkfed.org/newsevents/news/research/2026/20260811) in the second quarter of 2026. Auto loans: $1.71 trillion. Credit cards: $1.26 trillion, a hair under the record. Student loans: $1.65 trillion. The New York Fed's own words: new delinquencies for auto loans and credit cards "remain at elevated levels."

**Cars.** The average payment on a new car hit a record [$777 a month](https://www.carscoops.com/2026/07/car-loan-study-q2-2026/) this summer, the third straight quarterly record. One in five new-car buyers now pays more than $1,000 a month. Subprime auto delinquencies hit [6.9% in January](https://caredge.com/guides/auto-loan-crisis-32-year-record), the highest Fitch has recorded since 1994. Repossessions reached [1.73 million in 2024](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-03-26/car-repossessions-surge-americans-struggle-with-auto-loan-payments), the most since 2009, and Cox Automotive expects about 1.8 million a year through the end of the decade. In most of America, losing your car means losing your job.

**Student loans.** About [5.5 million borrowers are in default](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/student-loan-borrowers-default-wages-garnished-2026/). In January 2026 the government resumed garnishing their wages, up to 15% of take-home pay. Nobody suspended the rules for them for "a while."

**Groceries on credit.** [47% of buy-now-pay-later users paid late in the past year](https://www.lendingtree.com/personal/buy-now-pay-later-loan-statistics/), up from 34% two years ago. [29% are using it for groceries](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/14/buy-now-pay-later-bnpl-finance-groceries-rent-and-bills.html). Among Gen Z, 38%. More than half say they couldn't make ends meet without it.

**The floor.** The Federal Reserve's latest household survey found [37% of adults can't cover a $400 emergency with cash](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/other20260513a.htm). A record [22.7 million renter households are cost-burdened](https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/state-nations-housing-2026); 12.1 million pay more than half their income for housing. The number of apartments under $1,000 a month fell 30% in a decade.

Read those side by side. American Airlines: negative cash flow for a decade, $12.5 billion in buybacks, $54 billion rescue, CEO paid $13.9 million. A 24-year-old with a $777 car payment: one missed paycheck from a repo, and if she's got student loans, a garnishment notice.

Same country. Two sets of rules.

## What This Is Doing to America's Future

Here's why this matters beyond the outrage. Socialism for the rich isn't just unfair. It's expensive, it compounds, and the bill lands on the people who are youngest today.

**It's being paid for with borrowed money.** The national debt passed [$38 trillion](https://fortune.com/2025/12/17/38-trillion-national-debt-interest-payments-over-1-trillion-per-year-crfb-outlook-forecast/) in late 2025, more than the entire economy produces in a year. Net interest on that debt was $345 billion in 2020. In fiscal 2025 it crossed [$1 trillion](https://www.crfb.org/blogs/interest-debt-grow-past-1-trillion-next-year). We now spend more on interest than on Medicaid or national defense. The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget projects $1.8 trillion a year by 2035. Every bailout, every tax cut for the top decile, every guaranteed prison bed was borrowed. And interest on Treasury debt is itself a payment to bondholders, who are, overwhelmingly, the people who own assets. The Committee's phrase for this is exactly right: "servicing the past consumes an ever-growing share of the future."

**It's killing mobility.** In 1940, [92% of American kids grew up to earn more than their parents](https://opportunityinsights.org/paper/the-fading-american-dream/). For kids born in the 1980s, it's 50%. A coin flip. Young households' share of wealth has been cut nearly in half since 1989. The median first-time homebuyer is now [40 years old](https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/first-time-home-buyer-share-falls-to-historic-low-of-21-median-age-rises-to-40). Only [25% of non-homeowners](https://news.gallup.com/poll/708995/homebuying-intentions-decline-further.aspx) expect to buy in the next five years, the lowest Gallup has ever recorded. The fertility rate hit [a record low](https://www.cnn.com/2026/04/09/health/fertility-rate-record-low-2025) for the second year running. You cannot build a middle class when the down payment for one generation is the inheritance the last generation lost to a nursing home.

**It rewards the wrong risks.** When losses are socialized at the top, the rational move at the top is to take more risk. That's what a decade of buybacks followed by a bailout followed by record buybacks teaches. When losses are privatized at the bottom, the rational move at the bottom is to take *less* risk. Don't start the business. Don't move for the better job. Don't have the kid. An economy where the top gambles and the bottom hunkers down is not a dynamic economy. It's a rentier economy with a good PR department.

**It breaks trust.** Consumer sentiment is at 48.1. In the Fed's survey, only [73% of adults say they're doing okay](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/other20260513a.htm), down from 78% in 2021, and that's before the Medicaid cuts phase in. Mark Zandi at Moody's describes this economy as a house sitting on a few poles instead of a foundation. I'd add one thing. The poles are subsidized. The foundation isn't. A country that stops believing the rules are the same for everyone eventually stops playing by them. You can already see it in the politics, on both ends.

**It costs lives.** Between the nursing home staffing repeal, Medicaid cuts and SNAP cuts, the estimates range from about 1,500 to more than 51,000 preventable deaths a year. Even at the low end, that's a 9/11 every two years, paid for by a tax cut for the top decile.

The tell in all of this is the word "afford." We could afford $54 billion for airlines in two weeks, $20 billion for Argentina's peso and $45 billion for detention beds. We couldn't afford $186 billion for food over ten years. We could afford to guarantee every dollar in a Silicon Valley bank. We can't afford a nurse on every floor of a nursing home.

That's not a budget. That's a priority list.

## What I'd Actually Change

I don't think the answer is no bailouts, no Medicaid, no SNAP, no contracts. I think the answer is one rule, applied evenly: **if the public carries the risk, the public gets the upside, and the conditions don't expire.**

And we already know it can be done. In August 2025, the government put [$8.9 billion into Intel and took a 9.9% ownership stake](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/08/22/intel-goverment-equity-stake.html) in return. Whatever you think of that deal, the structure is right: taxpayers put in money, taxpayers own a piece. If we can do it for a chipmaker, we can do it for every airline, bank and company that ever asks for a rescue.

**Bailouts.** No buybacks or dividends for the life of the assistance, not for 18 months. Taxpayers hold equity, like Intel. The airlines proved that a temporary condition is just a delay.

**Long-term care.** Any facility taking Medicaid publishes every related-party payment in plain English, and the staffing floor comes back. If a fund wants to own nursing homes, its returns should depend on outcomes, not occupancy.

**Wages.** GAO already tells us which companies have the most full-time workers on food stamps. Put that number on the table every time a minimum wage bill comes up. A wage subsidy is a subsidy. Say so.

**Prisons.** No occupancy guarantees. No per-bed incentives. A contract that pays for empty cells is a contract that wants full ones.

**Debt.** Give the bottom of the K the same thing the top gets when it's in trouble: a pause and a path. If we can suspend the rules for an airline, we can cap the interest on a car loan.

None of this is radical. It's asking the top of the K to live under the same rules as the bottom.

## Why I Care About This

FinMango has now reached more than 100,000 students with financial education. I've spent years arguing that [financial health has to come before financial education](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1chxlQWPCe1QoFMuF146yqoETCujMlbEO_X324JyI_Sk/edit), and this is the reason.

You can teach a 17-year-old to save. You cannot teach her to out-save a system that takes her grandmother's house to pay a nursing home that hid its profits. You can teach a cashier to budget. You cannot budget your way out of a wage the government has to top up. You can teach a college kid about interest rates. You cannot teach him to out-earn a garnishment. Every one of these mechanisms takes money from the people I work with and moves it up the K. No spreadsheet fixes that.

## Bottom Line

King said it in 1968. We have socialism for the rich and rugged individualism for the poor. In 2026 the data says he was right, the gap is the widest it's ever been, and we just wrote the next round into law.

The bailout goes up. The bill comes down. And the bill is being sent to the next generation, with interest.

Until we change that, we're not fixing the K. We're funding it.
