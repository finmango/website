# We Already Have Socialism in America. It's Just for the Rich.

*Draft 1 — September 29, 2026. Every number below is linked to a primary or major-outlet source. A fact-check table and editorial notes are at the bottom.*

---

In 1968, Martin Luther King Jr. stood in front of a crowd at Hunter College and said something that still lands like a punch: ["This country has socialism for the rich, rugged individualism for the poor."](https://cityobservatory.org/dr-king-socialism-for-the-rich-and-rugged-free-enterprise-capitalism-for-the-poor/)

He was talking about suburbs built on federally subsidized credit and highways built with 90% federal money, while poor families were told to pull themselves up. When the government helped the rich, it was called a subsidy. When it helped the poor, it was called welfare.

Fifty-eight years later, the subsidies have changed. The pattern hasn't.

Here's the simplest way I can put it. When the people at the top of the economy take a risk and it blows up, the losses get spread across all of us. When the bet pays off, the profits stay private. When a family at the bottom takes a hit, they're on their own.

I've written before about why I think [the K-shaped economy is here to stay](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1mkVJuvFGPEtQxVfwWNF8Ps4chrGmZAkVYmjX1YKaPU4/edit). This piece is about the machine underneath it. Because the K didn't happen by accident. A lot of it is policy. And a lot of that policy moves money in exactly one direction.

I'm not anti-business. I run a nonprofit and I've built a company. I'm not selling fear either. I just think we should call things what they are.

## Quick Background

If you need the K-shape refresher, here it is in four numbers.

The top 1% of American households now hold [32.5% of all U.S. wealth](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/WFRBST01134), per the Federal Reserve's latest data (Q2 2026). That's a new record. The bottom 50%? [2.3%](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/WFRBSB50215). Combined.

The share of economic output going to workers just hit [52.8%](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2026/labor-share-at-its-lowest-level-52-8-percent-in-second-quarter-2026.htm), the lowest level since the Bureau of Labor Statistics started tracking it in 1947. Back then it was 65.5%.

And the stock market, which keeps hitting records? The top 1% own [about half of all corporate equities and mutual fund shares](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/z1/dataviz/dfa/distribute/table/). The bottom 50% own about 1%.

Those are the outcomes. Now let's talk about the mechanisms. There are five of them, and once you see the pattern you can't unsee it.

## 1. The Airlines: Heads They Win, Tails You Pay

Let's start with the cleanest example in modern American history.

In the decade before the pandemic, the biggest U.S. airlines spent [96% of their free cash flow buying back their own stock](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2020-03-16/u-s-airlines-spent-96-of-free-cash-flow-on-buybacks-chart). American Airlines had *negative* cumulative free cash flow over that decade and still repurchased [more than $12.5 billion](https://www.dallasnews.com/business/airlines/2020/03/18/american-airlines-spent-12-billion-on-stock-buybacks-during-flush-times-now-it-says-it-needs-a-bailout/) of its own shares. Buybacks push the stock price up. Stock price is how executives get paid. That's the whole game.

Then COVID hit. Within weeks, Congress created the Payroll Support Program. Three rounds later, passenger airlines had received [$54 billion](https://home.treasury.gov/policy-issues/coronavirus/assistance-for-american-industry/payroll-support-program) in federal support. The only real string attached: no buybacks or dividends for a while.

"A while" ended in the fall of 2022. Southwest [reinstated its dividend in December 2022](https://www.marketbeat.com/articles/southwest-airlines-brings-back-dividend-as-travel-rebounds-2022-12-07). Delta [brought its dividend back in June 2023](https://www.ajc.com/news/atlanta-airport-blog/delta-to-resume-dividend-payouts-with-cares-act-restrictions-lifted/FJW5E4D4FBEOBGF3GPCSERKOIY/). United announced a [$1.5 billion buyback in October 2024](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/10/15/united-airlines-ual-3q-2024-earnings.html), its first since before the pandemic. The unions [begged them not to](https://www.cnbc.com/2022/08/18/airline-unions-urge-carriers-not-to-resume-buybacks-when-bailout-ban-ends-this-fall.html). They did it anyway.

In 2025, United's CEO made [$32.3 million](https://onemileatatime.com/insights/highest-paid-airline-ceo/). Delta's made $19.2 million. American posted [record revenue of $54.6 billion](https://liveandletsfly.com/american-airlines-profit-plunges-premium-pivot-2025/).

Meanwhile, Spirit Airlines, one of the cheapest ways for a working-class family to fly, [shut down in May 2026](https://www.cnn.com/2026/05/02/business/spirit-to-halt-all-flights) and took 17,000 jobs with it. Nobody wrote Spirit a check.

And the airlines weren't even the biggest version of this. The Paycheck Protection Program handed out roughly $800 billion. Economists led by MIT's David Autor found that only [23 to 34 cents of every PPP dollar](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fjep.36.2.55) went to workers who would otherwise have lost their jobs. The rest went to business owners, shareholders, creditors and suppliers. Of the 2020 money, [72% flowed to the top fifth of households by income](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fjep.36.2.55). The cost per job-year saved: $169,000 to $258,000.

Then in March 2023, Silicon Valley Bank collapsed. Deposits are insured up to $250,000. Overnight, the government [guaranteed every dollar](https://www.fdic.gov/news/press-releases/2023/pr23019.html), including the uninsured balances of venture-backed startups and wealthy depositors. The [$16.3 billion](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2023/11/29/2023-25813/special-assessment-pursuant-to-systemic-risk-determination) bill got charged to the banking system, which means it got charged to bank customers. Which means it got charged to you.

Here's the thing. Every one of these bailouts had a defensible argument in the moment. I'm not saying let the planes fall out of the sky. I'm saying notice how fast the money moved, and who it landed on.

## 2. The Affordability Crisis: Rugged Individualism for Everyone Else

Now flip the K over and look at the bottom leg.

The Federal Reserve's latest household survey, fielded in October 2025, found that [37% of American adults could not cover a $400 emergency expense with cash](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/other20260513a.htm). Four hundred dollars. The share who say they're "doing okay" financially has dropped from 78% in 2021 to 73%.

Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies reports a record [22.7 million renter households are cost-burdened](https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/state-nations-housing-2026), meaning more than 30% of income goes to housing. That's 49% of all renters. 12.1 million pay more than half. The number of apartments renting for under $1,000 a month [fell 30% between 2014 and 2024](https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/state-nations-housing-2026), from 8.3 million to 5.8 million. For renters earning under $30,000, [83% are cost-burdened](https://nlihc.org/resource/jchss-2026-state-nations-housing-report-finds-cost-burdens-renters-continue-rise-amid).

Credit card balances sit at [$1.26 trillion](https://www.newyorkfed.org/newsevents/news/research/2026/20260811), a hair under last year's all-time high. Total household debt: $18.8 trillion.

Homelessness in January 2025 was [745,652 people](https://www.hud.gov/news/hud-no-26-037). That's the first year-over-year decline since 2016, which is genuinely good news. It's also still 27% higher than 2013 and the second-highest count on record.

And the New York Fed just confirmed what the K looks like in real spending. Since 2023, growth has been [driven almost entirely by households earning more than $125,000](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/05/tracking-the-k-shaped-economy-whos-driving-spending/). Households under $40,000 saw their real spending *fall* for parts of 2023 and didn't get back to where they started until mid-2024. The Fed's own words: "recent growth in consumption has not been broad based."

So what did Washington do for the bottom leg of the K? The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed in July 2025, cuts roughly [$1 trillion from Medicaid](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61534) and [$186 billion from SNAP](https://www.cbpp.org/research/food-assistance/by-the-numbers-senate-republican-leaderships-reconciliation-bill-takes) through 2034. That's about 20% of the food program, the largest cut in its history. CBO estimates [3.2 million adults, plus 2 million children in their households](https://www.cbpp.org/research/food-assistance/by-the-numbers-senate-republican-leaderships-reconciliation-bill-takes), will lose some or all of their food benefits from the new work requirements alone.

Read those two sections back to back. $54 billion for airlines took about two weeks. $800 billion in PPP went out in months. Food assistance for kids got a ten-year cut because "we can't afford it."

That's not a budget. That's a priority list.

## 3. Long-Term Care: The Quietest Wealth Transfer in America

This is the one almost nobody talks about, and it's the one that hits the working and middle class hardest.

More than half of Americans turning 65 today, [56%](https://rooseveltinstitute.org/publications/how-long-term-care-costs-drain-the-middle-class/), will need long-term care at some point. One in five will need it for five years or more.

A private room in a nursing home now costs a median of [$129,575 a year](https://investor.genworth.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/1054/carescout-releases-2025-cost-of-care-survey-results). Median household income for Americans over 65 is about $57,000.

The math doesn't work, so here's what actually happens. Families pay privately until the money runs out. Then they qualify for Medicaid, which in most states requires you to have [no more than $2,000 in countable assets](https://www.medicaidlongtermcare.org/eligibility/spend-down/). A 2025 study of nursing home residents found that among those who entered paying privately, [one in six spent down to Medicaid during their stay, in an average of just 6.1 months](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12679321/). The Roosevelt Institute found that [more than four out of five middle-class adults who need five-plus years of care end up on Medicaid](https://rooseveltinstitute.org/publications/how-long-term-care-costs-drain-the-middle-class/). After care begins, middle-class families keep about 42% of their wealth. The top quarter keeps 94%.

Then, when you die, federal law [requires states to recover what Medicaid spent from your estate](https://www.medicaid.gov/medicaid/eligibility-policy/estate-recovery). For most working-class families, the estate is the house. The one asset they had to pass down.

So who's on the other end of that transfer?

Medicaid is the primary payer for [63% of nursing home residents](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/5-key-facts-about-nursing-facilities-and-medicaid/). It paid 61% of the $459 billion the country spent on long-term care in 2023. That's public money, flowing to mostly private operators. And a lot of it doesn't stay in the building.

Nearly three-quarters of U.S. nursing homes, more than 11,000, do business with [companies owned by the same people who own the nursing home](https://kffhealthnews.org/aging/care-suffers-as-more-nursing-homes-feed-money-into-corporate-webs/). The home pays "rent" to its owner's real estate company. It pays "management fees" to its owner's management company. In 2015 alone that was $11 billion, a tenth of all nursing home spending. Homes that do this have fewer nurses per patient, higher injury rates, and almost twice the complaints. A 2024 NBER paper found that nursing homes hide [at least 63% of their profits](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w32258/w32258.pdf) this way. The books say the home is barely breaking even. The owner's other companies say otherwise.

It gets worse with private equity. A landmark study of more than 7 million Medicare patients found that when a PE firm buys a nursing home, [short-term mortality goes up 10%](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28474). About 21,000 lives over the study period. At the same time, spending per patient went up 19%, "the vast majority of which is billed to taxpayers."

Let that sink in. They got paid more. The patients died more. And the check came from the public.

In 2024, the federal government finally set a minimum staffing standard: at least 3.48 hours of nursing care per resident per day. The industry's main lobby, the American Health Care Association, [spent nearly $17 million lobbying since 2020](https://www.warren.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/warren-schakowsky-slam-largest-nursing-home-lobbying-groups-for-sabotaging-biden-administrations-minimum-nurse-staffing-rules-that-improve-quality-of-care-for-seniors) against it. In December 2025, [the rule was repealed](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/12/03/2025-21792/medicare-and-medicaid-programs-repeal-of-minimum-staffing-standards-for-long-term-care-facilities).

Follow the money one more time. Grandma's house becomes Medicaid's reimbursement. Medicaid's reimbursement becomes the operator's revenue. The operator's revenue becomes rent to the owner's real estate company. The real estate company is owned by a fund. The fund's investors are, overwhelmingly, the top of the K.

They get paid whether the care is good or not. Actually, the data says they get paid *more* when it isn't.

## 4. Grocery Stores and SNAP: Subsidized at Both Ends

SNAP is one of the most effective anti-hunger programs in the world. I want to be clear about that up front. In fiscal 2025 it helped [42.1 million people a month](https://www.ers.usda.gov/topics/food-nutrition-assistance/supplemental-nutrition-assistance-program-snap/key-statistics-and-research) with an average benefit of $188 per person. Total cost: [$101.7 billion](https://usafacts.org/answers/how-much-does-the-federal-government-spend-on-snap-every-year/country/united-states/).

Now look at where those dollars go.

Walmart captures [roughly a quarter of all SNAP grocery spending](https://www.grocerydive.com/news/snap-grocery-spending-5-big-numbers/804627/), triple Kroger's share. Do the math: about a quarter of $95 billion in benefits is somewhere around $23 billion a year moving through Walmart's registers. Walmart's most recent fiscal year: [$713 billion in revenue and $22.3 billion in profit](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/104169/000010416926000032/earningsreleasefy26q4.htm). The Walton family, which owns about 44% of the company, is worth [$475 billion](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/01/16/walton-family-net-worth-rises-32-billion-this-year/).

That's one end. Here's the other.

In June 2026, the Government Accountability Office published its second study of who employs America's safety-net recipients. In 2024, [13.8 million working adults were on Medicaid and 10.6 million were in working SNAP households](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108703). Two-thirds worked full-time. These aren't people who won't work. They're people whose full-time job doesn't pay enough to eat.

And who employs them? Across the nine states GAO could get SNAP data from, [Walmart topped the list with 15,515 workers on food stamps](https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/08/13/grocery-retailers-scrutinized-over-employee-public-assistance/), followed by Amazon, Dollar General, Dollar Tree and Kroger. Amazon's count nearly tripled since 2020. Senator Sanders' office, which commissioned the report, noted that Walmart's profits rose about 47% since 2020 while Medicaid enrollment among its workers rose 55%.

So here's the loop. A cashier's wages don't cover groceries. Taxpayers cover the gap through SNAP. The cashier walks to the front of the store and spends that SNAP money at the register. Taxpayers have now subsidized the payroll *and* the sales of the same company. Twice.

And when Congress cut SNAP by $186 billion, the cut landed on the cashier. Not on the margin.

I'm not saying Walmart designed it this way. I'm saying that when a company can pay below the cost of living and the public fills the gap, the public is doing the company's payroll for it. There's a word for a government program that props up private profit. It just isn't the word we use when the recipient is rich.

## 5. Private Prisons: Guaranteed Customers, Guaranteed Revenue

Every business wants guaranteed demand. Only one industry has it written into a government contract.

When In the Public Interest analyzed more than 60 private prison contracts, [65% contained occupancy guarantees](https://inthepublicinterest.org/criminal-how-lockup-quotas-and-low-crime-taxes-guarantee-profits-for-private-prison-corporations/). The most common: 90%. Three Arizona prisons: 100%. If crime falls and beds go empty, the state pays for them anyway. They call it a "low-crime tax." Think about the incentive that creates.

Immigration detention is where the model has gone fully vertical. As of early 2025, [86% of ICE detainees were held in for-profit facilities](https://tracreports.org/reports/753/). The One Big Beautiful Bill Act added [$45 billion for detention](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/budget-bill-massively-increases-funding-immigration-detention), a 265% increase, enough for roughly 116,000 beds by 2029. More than the entire federal prison system.

The two biggest contractors had the best year in their history.

GEO Group's 2025 revenue hit [$2.63 billion, and net income went from $32 million in 2024 to $254 million](https://investors.geogroup.com/news-releases/news-release-details/geo-group-reports-fourth-quarter-and-full-year-2025-results). Its founder called it "the largest amount of new business we have won in a single year in our company's history." ICE detainees in GEO facilities: about 24,000, "the highest level we have ever had." Ankle monitors under its contract went from 17,000 to more than 42,000.

CoreCivic's federal revenue [rose 19% to $1.19 billion](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1070985/000119312526060669/cxw-20251231.htm). Its ICE revenue in the fourth quarter alone [more than doubled](https://theappeal.org/ice-geo-group-corecivic-profits/). Its CEO told shareholders: "Never in our 42-year company history have we had so much activity and demand for our services."

Both companies gave [$500,000 to the 2025 inaugural committee](https://abcnews.com/US/private-prison-firm-corecivic-gave-500k-trumps-inauguration/story?id=118218707), double what they gave in 2017. Together with their PACs and subsidiaries, they donated [$2.78 million](https://www.citizensforethics.org/reports-investigations/crew-investigations/trumps-budget-bill-benefits-private-immigration-detention-companies-that-donated-to-trump/) to the campaign, the inaugural and related entities. Then the budget passed. Then the stocks went up.

Inside the walls, incarcerated workers nationwide earn [an average of 13 to 52 cents an hour](https://www.aclu.org/publications/captive-labor-exploitation-incarcerated-workers). Seven states pay nothing at all.

This isn't a market. A market has customers who can walk away. This is a public agency guaranteeing a private company's demand with taxpayer money, and the product is a human being in a cell.

## The Pattern

Line them up.

Airlines: the public carries the risk, shareholders keep the return.
Long-term care: the public pays the bill, related-party owners keep the profit, families lose the house.
SNAP: the public covers the payroll gap, then hands the money back at the register.
Prisons: the public guarantees the demand, contractors guarantee their margin.

In every case the money moves the same direction. Toward people who own assets. And we know who owns assets: the top 1% own half the stock market and the bottom half of the country owns 1%. When the government backstops a company, it's backstopping that company's shareholders. That's not a bailout of "the economy." That's a bailout of the top of the K.

The tell is the word "afford." We could afford $54 billion for airlines in two weeks and $45 billion for detention beds. We couldn't afford $186 billion for food over ten years. We could afford to guarantee every dollar in a Silicon Valley bank. We can't afford a nurse on every floor of a nursing home.

## What This Does to the Future of America

Here's why this matters beyond the outrage.

The deal in this country used to be simple. Work hard, play by the rules, and the risks you take are yours, but so are the rewards. That deal only holds if it's the same deal for everyone. Right now it isn't. Risk is socialized at the top and privatized at the bottom.

And young people can tell. The median first-time homebuyer is now [40 years old](https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/first-time-home-buyer-share-falls-to-historic-low-of-21-median-age-rises-to-40). Only [25% of non-homeowners](https://news.gallup.com/poll/708995/homebuying-intentions-decline-further.aspx) expect to buy in the next five years, the lowest Gallup has ever recorded. The fertility rate hit [a record low](https://www.cnn.com/2026/04/09/health/fertility-rate-record-low-2025) for the second year running. Americans under 30 rank [62nd in the world](https://worldhappiness.report/) in happiness.

Those aren't separate stories. They're what happens when a generation looks at the rules, realizes the safety net is above them and the trapdoor is below them, and starts making decisions accordingly.

Mark Zandi at Moody's describes this economy as a house sitting on a few poles instead of a foundation. I'd add one thing. The poles are subsidized. The foundation isn't.

## What I'd Actually Change

I don't think the answer is no bailouts, no Medicaid, no SNAP, no contracts. I think the answer is one rule, applied evenly: **if the public carries the risk, the public gets the upside, and the conditions don't expire.**

**Bailouts.** No buybacks or dividends for the life of the assistance, not for 18 months. If taxpayers save a company, taxpayers should hold equity in it. The airlines proved that a temporary condition is just a delay.

**Long-term care.** Any facility taking Medicaid should have to publish every related-party payment in plain English, and the staffing floor should come back. If a fund wants to own nursing homes, its returns should depend on outcomes, not occupancy.

**Wages.** GAO already tells us which companies have the most full-time workers on food stamps. Put that number on the table every time a minimum wage bill comes up. A wage subsidy is a subsidy. Say so.

**Prisons.** No occupancy guarantees. No per-bed incentives. A contract that pays for empty cells is a contract that wants full ones.

None of this is radical. It's just asking the top of the K to live under the same rules as the bottom.

## Why I Care About This

FinMango has now reached more than 100,000 students with financial education. I've spent years arguing that [financial health has to come before financial education](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1chxlQWPCe1QoFMuF146yqoETCujMlbEO_X324JyI_Sk/edit), and this is the reason.

You can teach a 17-year-old to save. You cannot teach her to out-save a system that takes her grandmother's house to pay a nursing home that hid its profits. You can teach a cashier to budget. You cannot budget your way out of a wage the government has to top up. Every one of these mechanisms takes money from the people I work with and moves it up the K. No spreadsheet fixes that.

## Bottom Line

King said it in 1968. We have socialism for the rich and rugged individualism for the poor. In 2026 the data says he was right, and the gap is wider than it's ever been.

The bailout goes up. The bill comes down. Until we change that, we're not fixing the K. We're funding it.

## EDITORIAL NOTES (delete before publishing)

**Word count:** about 2,900 words for the piece itself. Your K-shaped post ran about 1,900. If you want it tighter for LinkedIn, the SVB paragraph, the "What This Does to the Future" section and the prison-labor line are the easiest cuts. For a newspaper op-ed (700–900 words) I'd keep the intro, one case study (long-term care is the freshest), the "afford" paragraph and the bottom line.

**Alternate titles:**
- "Socialism for the Rich Is Alive and Well. Just Look at the Back Pages."
- "The Bailout Goes Up. The Bill Comes Down."
- "The K-Shaped Economy Isn't an Accident"

**Numbers I deliberately did NOT use:**
- The "top 10% of earners drive 49.7% of consumer spending" Moody's stat. Moody's revised it down to 45.8% after a methodology change, Zandi himself said he "wouldn't die on that hill," and the BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey puts it near 23%. The New York Fed Liberty Street numbers say the same thing more defensibly.
- "Nursing homes get paid even after they make people broke" as a literal claim. The defensible version is what's in the draft: Medicaid keeps paying after spend-down, and PE-owned homes billed 19% more while mortality rose 10%.
- Any claim that Walmart "lobbies to keep SNAP so it can pay less." I couldn't find hard evidence of that causal claim. The draft states the two verified facts (share of SNAP dollars, number of employees on SNAP) and lets the reader connect them.

**Things to double-check before publishing:**
- The Fed DFA numbers (32.5% / 2.3%) are Q2 2026, released September 18, 2026. Your May piece used 31.7%. The new figure is a fresh record, which strengthens the story.
- The GAO report (GAO-26-108703) was reissued with revisions on August 24, 2026. The employer counts in the draft come from FoodNavigator's summary of the reissued version. Worth pulling the PDF table directly.
- "$1 trillion from Medicaid" is the widely cited rounding of CBO's estimate for the enacted bill (you used the same rounding in the K-shaped piece). If an editor pushes, pull the exact ten-year Medicaid line from CBO's final cost estimate and cite it directly.
- The SVB paragraph says the cost "got charged to you." Technically the $16.3B special assessment hit ~114 large banks. The pass-through to customers is an inference. Soften to "charged to the banking system" if you want zero exposure.
- The "median household income for Americans over 65 is about $57,000" figure comes from the Roosevelt Institute brief (April 2026). Their source is Census CPS.

**Tone check:** I kept your rhythm (short paragraphs, "Let that sink in," "Read that back," "Here's the thing," a "Quick Background," a "Why I Care" and a "Bottom Line"). I did not reuse "swimming in a riptide" since you've used it in two published pieces. "The bailout goes up. The bill comes down." is the new spine line if you want one.
