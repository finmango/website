## Instagram caption

Latin America treats women's economic autonomy as a social agenda. It should
treat it as infrastructure — the invisible kind that decides whether everything
else in an economy works.

$6.7 trillion. That's what gender gaps in lifetime earnings cost Latin America
and the Caribbean in human capital wealth. Half of employed women in the region
work informally. Unpaid care work is worth 21.4% of regional GDP and appears in
no national account. Only 66% of women have a financial account, against 74% of
men — and the gap widens when money goes digital.

These aren't three separate problems. They compound.

Ambassador Lizbeth Sinaí Rodríguez (Crefemme) spent 2024 running Female
Financial Autonomy in Sayula, Jalisco — 50 women, one month, almost no attrition.
The most useful finding wasn't about financial tools at all: the binding
constraints were trust, risk perception, networks, and norms about who is
permitted to make economic decisions. Several participants had never approached a
bank — not for lack of information, but because they expected to be rejected.

By 2038, for the first time in the region's history, people over 60 will equal
children under 14. If there will be fewer workers tomorrow, can the region
afford to underuse the ones it already has?

## LinkedIn version

We picture infrastructure as highways, ports, and networks. There is another
kind, far less visible, that shapes the growth capacity of Latin America's
economies: women's economic autonomy.

FinMango Ambassador Lizbeth Sinaí Rodríguez (Crefemme — Financial Education for
Women) makes the case that the region's framing is the problem. Treated as an
equity agenda, autonomy competes with growth priorities. Treated as
infrastructure, it *is* one.

The numbers behind the argument:

• $6.7T — the human capital wealth Latin America and the Caribbean lose to
  gender gaps in lifetime earnings (World Bank, 2018)
• ~50% of employed women in the region work informally (OECD, 2025)
• 21.4% of regional GDP is unpaid care work, recorded nowhere (UN Women, 2025)
• 66% of women hold a financial account vs 74% of men; 55% vs 64% for digital
  payments (FinDev Gateway, 2025)

And the mechanism that makes them compound: a woman without an account cannot
build credit history; without credit history she cannot access financing;
without financing her business stays small.

What the field added to the macro picture: running Female Financial Autonomy for
50 women in Sayula, Jalisco, the binding constraint turned out not to be
information. It was the reasonable expectation of being rejected. The single most
consequential design decision was ending the program with participants pitching
their business plans to a committee that included the U.S. Consul General in
Guadalajara — attendance and preparation rose sharply, because someone with
institutional standing was going to take their business seriously.

Three shifts follow: design products for people without credit history rather
than around them; pair training with access to a product, market, or network;
and treat care infrastructure as a labor supply intervention, not a welfare
expense.
