Calculator · Inflation
One flat yearly rate, compounded · Presets 2%, 3% and 4.1%

What will your money be worth later?

Put in an amount, pick a year, and see what it would take to buy the same things then. The rate is yours to set. 3% is close to the long-run US average. Try 2% and 4.1% as well, because the gap between them is most of the story.

$
Any amount works. The result scales with it.
%
US inflation has averaged about 3% a year over the long run. The Fed aims for 2%. Source: BLS CPI and FRED.

In 2056 you'd need about $243 to buy what $100 buys today. Over those 30 years a dollar from today shrinks to about $0.41, and prices double roughly every 23 years.

How this is worked out

Future cost is today's price times (1 + rate) to the power of the number of years. That is the ordinary compound formula, run with one flat rate. "What a dollar shrinks to" is the inverse, so it shows how much of today's buying power one dollar keeps.

The 3% preset is the rough long-run US average since 1925, per BLS CPI data. The 2% preset is the Federal Reserve's target. "Prices double every" uses ln(2) divided by ln(1 + rate), the time it takes buying power to drop by half.

This ignores wage growth, investment returns, and the fact that some categories run hotter than the headline rate. Healthcare, college and housing have usually risen faster than CPI. Nothing you type leaves this page.

Why this matters

Cash isn't a strategy. It's a slow leak.

Inflation isn't loud. It doesn't show up on a bill. But over a working lifetime it quietly cuts what your money can do, and most people don't plan for it.

~96%

What a 1913 dollar buys today

The US dollar has lost roughly 96% of its buying power since the Bureau of Labor Statistics began tracking CPI in 1913. The dollar that bought a meal then buys a few cents of groceries today. Source: BLS inflation calculator.

What inflation actually is

Inflation is the rate at which prices rise across the economy. When it runs at 3%, a basket of goods that costs $100 today costs about $103 a year from now.

Your salary, your savings and your investments all have to outrun it just to stay in place.

How fast cash halves

Divide 72 by the inflation rate and you get the rough number of years for prices to double, or for cash to lose half its value.

At 3% that is about 24 years. At 6%, just 12. A savings account earning 0% is quietly melting in the background.

Outrun it, don't fight it

The S&P 500 has returned roughly 10% a year on average over the last century, per S&P data, well above long-run inflation.

You can't stop inflation. You just need your money somewhere that grows faster than prices do.
